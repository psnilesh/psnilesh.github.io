Reference

What implied volatility is

Volatility measures how much a stock bounces around, in either direction, expressed as a percentage per year. It says nothing about direction: 30% volatility does not mean the stock is expected to gain 30%, it means the stock is expected to end the year within roughly ±30% of today about two thirds of the time.

Nobody publishes a volatility number. What you see is a price. An option formula turns stock price, strike, time and volatility into a price, so traders run it backwards and ask which volatility figure reproduces the price being quoted. That answer is the implied volatility. It is the market's forecast and a price tag at the same time, which is why it is the right way to compare a $3 option on a $100 stock against a $12 option on a $400 one.

Why volatility scales with the square root of time

Suppose a stock moves exactly ±8.66% each month on a coin flip. After twelve months it is not 12 × 8.66% = 104% away from where it started, because a typical year delivers about six up months and six down months that largely cancel.

For independent moves, variances add but standard deviations do not, so the typical distance after n steps of size d is d × sqrt(n). Twelve months of 8.66% composes to 8.66% × sqrt(12) = 30% for the year. Running it backwards, one month of a 30% annual volatility is 30% / sqrt(12), which is 8.66%, not 30% / 12.

Dividing by 12 would assume no cancellation at all. The square root sits between "every move adds up" and "every move cancels", and the table above is just that arithmetic at five horizons. The same relationship gives the trader's shortcut: annual IV divided by sqrt(252) ≈ 16 is a typical day's move.

It also explains why a three-month option costs nowhere near three times a one-month option. It costs about sqrt(3) ≈ 1.7 times as much, so longer-dated options are cheap per unit of time.

Reading the Greeks

Delta is how many dollars the option gains per $1 the stock gains. It doubles as a rough probability of finishing in the money, so 0.52 is close to a coin flip. Calls run 0 to 1, puts 0 to −1.

Gamma is delta's own rate of change, and it is the reason large moves pay disproportionately. At $100 your delta might be 0.52; by $105 it is 0.73, so each dollar earns more than the last. Gamma is largest at the money and grows enormous near expiry.

Theta is what you lose per day with the stock unchanged. It accelerates as expiry approaches, because the remaining time value tracks sqrt(T), and sqrt(T) falls off a cliff at the end: 30 days to 15 days costs 29% of it, 4 days to 1 day costs half.

Vega is what one point of implied volatility is worth. It is the one that bites when nothing visible happens. Rho is interest rate sensitivity and barely matters on short-dated trades.

Delta is not fixed as volatility changes. Higher IV pulls every delta toward 0.50; lower IV pushes them out toward 0 and 1. So a volatility collapse both cuts your position value and reduces how much you earn per dollar of recovery.

Why the broker's breakeven is the wrong number for a seller

The breakeven a broker displays is strike + premium for a call and strike - premium for a put. It is arithmetic rather than a model, and it is volatility-independent by construction, because at expiry every Greek has decayed to nothing and the option is worth exactly its intrinsic value.

If you intend to sell rather than exercise, that number is too pessimistic: selling early also recovers whatever time value is left, so your real breakeven is lower. It is also silent about the thing most likely to hurt you, which is the volatility drop you absorb in the first minutes after a scheduled event. The volatility crush, and why being right is not enough

Before an announcement nobody knows the outcome, so the market prices in a large move and implied volatility is high. Once the news is out the uncertainty is resolved, the rest of the option's life is ordinary trading, and volatility falls back to normal. Being long options means being long vega, so that fall costs you money regardless of direction.

Worked example: a $100 stock with earnings tomorrow, a 30-day $100 call at 70% IV costing about $8.05. Earnings land well, the stock rises 3% to $103, and IV reverts to 35%. The call is now worth about $5.77. You called the direction correctly and lost 28%. The stock needed roughly $106.40, about a 6.4% move, just to break even, and that 6.4% figure is approximately what the market charged you for.

Scheduled events do this reliably: earnings, FDA decisions, court rulings, elections, rate decisions, merger votes. The tell is simple. Compare the option's current IV against its usual level for that stock. If it is double, something is priced in and you are paying for it.

What this model assumes, and where it is wrong

This is Black-Scholes, which prices European options: exercise only at expiry. US single-stock options are American style and can be exercised early, which makes a real American put worth slightly more than shown here. For the buy-to-sell trading this page is aimed at, the difference is usually small; for deep in-the-money puts, or calls on a stock about to pay a large dividend, it is not.

It also assumes returns are lognormally distributed and volatility is a single constant number. Real markets have fat tails and a volatility smile, meaning different strikes trade at different implied volatilities. Enter the IV for the specific strike you are looking at rather than one figure for the whole chain.

The dividend input is a continuous yield, which suits an index. Single stocks pay discrete dividends on specific dates, and that is not modelled.

Nothing here accounts for the bid-ask spread, which for an illiquid option quoted $3.20 / $3.70 costs about 15% round-trip and will swamp every refinement on this page. Check the spread before you check anything else.
